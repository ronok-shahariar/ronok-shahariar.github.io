<!-- =========================================================
     QUESTION
========================================================= -->

<section>

<p class="research-label">The question that started the research</p>

<div class="research-question">
Can a generalized fading model provide a common way to study different wireless channel conditions?
</div>

<p class="research-answer">
Wireless channels behave differently depending on fading, distance, and propagation conditions. I wanted to understand whether a generalized model could provide a common framework for studying those variations — and then use that framework to answer a practical question: <strong>how secure is the wireless channel when eavesdroppers can appear at random locations?</strong>
</p>

</section>


<!-- =========================================================
     WHY SECRECY
========================================================= -->

<section>

<p class="research-label">Why secrecy?</p>

<h2>Why does channel secrecy matter?</h2>

<p class="research-answer">
Wireless communication takes place over an open medium. A signal intended for a legitimate receiver may also be observed by an unintended listener. This means that channel conditions influence not only communication reliability, but also how difficult it is for an eavesdropper to intercept the transmission.
</p>

<!-- =========================================================
     SYSTEM MODEL
========================================================= -->

<section>

<p class="research-label">The system I studied</p>

<h2>What did I model?</h2>

<p class="research-answer">
I studied a <strong>dual-hop wireless network</strong> where confidential data travels from a source to a destination through an intermediate relay. The legitimate links and the eavesdropper links were modeled using the generalized <strong>α−µ fading model</strong>, while eavesdroppers could appear at random locations around the network.
</p>

     METRICS

<section>

<p class="research-label">Measuring secrecy</p>

<h2>How did I measure whether the communication was secure?</h2>

<p class="research-answer">
Instead of relying on a single measure, I evaluated the network using four complementary secrecy metrics.
</p>

<div class="metric-grid">

  <div class="metric-card">
    <strong>SOP</strong>
    <span>
      How likely secure communication is to fall below the required secrecy level.
    </span>
  </div>

  <div class="metric-card">
    <strong>SPSC</strong>
    <span>
      Whether a positive secure communication rate can be maintained.
    </span>
  </div>

  <div class="metric-card">
    <strong>IP</strong>
    <span>
      The probability that an eavesdropper can successfully intercept information.
    </span>
  </div>

  <div class="metric-card">
    <strong>EST</strong>
    <span>
      The useful confidential throughput remaining after secrecy outages are considered.
    </span>
  </div>

</div>

     FINDINGS

<section>

<p class="research-label">Main findings</p>

<h2>What did the analysis reveal?</h2>

<div class="findings-grid">

  <div class="finding">

    <span class="finding-number">01</span>

    <h3>Both hops exposed = highest risk</h3>

    <p>
      Simultaneous interception of the source and relay links produced the most critical secrecy condition among the three scenarios.
    </p>

  </div>


  <div class="finding">

    <span class="finding-number">02</span>

    <h3>Stronger legitimate links improve secrecy</h3>

    <p>
      Better legitimate-channel conditions and receive diversity reduce secrecy outage and improve secure communication performance.
    </p>

  </div>


  <div class="finding">

    <span class="finding-number">03</span>

    <h3>Eavesdropper distribution matters</h3>

    <p>
      As the density of randomly distributed eavesdroppers increases, the probability of successful interception also increases.
    </p>

  </div>

<section>

<p class="research-label">For readers who want the mathematics</p>

<h2>The analytical framework</h2>

<p class="research-answer">
The complete derivation includes the α−µ channel statistics, spatial modeling of randomly distributed eavesdroppers, PDF/CDF formulations, and closed-form secrecy expressions.
</p>


<details class="technical-details">

<summary>
View mathematical formulation
</summary>

<div class="technical-body" markdown="1">


### Generalized α−µ Channel Model

For a SIMO link, the instantaneous SNR distribution was modeled using the α−µ generalized fading model:

$$
f_{sr}(x)
=
\beta_1 x^{\beta_2}
e^{-\beta_3 x^{\bar{\alpha}_r}}
$$

where the model parameters represent the fading characteristics, receive diversity, and average SNR of the legitimate channel.


### Spatially Distributed Eavesdroppers

Random eavesdropper locations were incorporated into the wiretap-channel statistics using a spatial distribution model. The resulting formulation captures the relationship between eavesdropper density, path loss, fading, and received wiretap SNR.


### Secrecy Outage Probability

The secrecy outage probability evaluates the likelihood that the communication cannot maintain the required secrecy rate:

$$
SOP
=
\int_{0}^{\infty}
F_{\text{legitimate}}(x)
f_{\text{wiretap}}(x)
\,dx
$$

For simultaneous interception across both hops:

$$
SOP_3
=
S_1 + S_2 - S_1S_2
$$


### Strictly Positive Secrecy Capacity

$$
SPSC_3
=
(1-\mu_1)(1-\mu_2)
$$


### Intercept Probability

$$
IP = 1-SPSC
$$


### Effective Secrecy Throughput

$$
EST
=
R_s(1-SOP)
$$


The complete derivations and closed-form expressions are available in the full thesis.

</div>

</details>
